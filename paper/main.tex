\begin{abstract}
Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) planning requires accurate localization of aortic root landmarks from pre-procedural CT to assess complication risks and guide device selection. Existing methods detect only a partial landmark set in a fully supervised setting, leaving coronary obstruction risk assessment outside the automated pipeline, while requiring complete annotation of training data---a practical bottleneck for 3D CT volumes. We propose TRUST (Topological Reasoning and Uncertainty-aware Semi-supervised Teaching), a two-stage framework that detects 10 aortic root landmarks---including the coronary ostia for coronary safety assessment---under semi-supervised conditions. The key design principle is unified pool-based uncertainty: a single per-landmark estimate, derived from a cross-student test-time augmentation (TTA) pool and decomposed via the law of total variance into a within-student TTA-perturbation variance and a cross-student disagreement term, acts as a \emph{training-time} control signal that simultaneously weights the cross pseudo supervision loss, conditions the diffusivity of an uncertainty-guided heat conduction operator (UG-HCO) for adaptive feature propagation, and gates message passing in a topological graph convolutional network (Topo-GCN) for structural refinement. On a dataset of 150 labeled and 620 unlabeled aortic root CT volumes (100 training, 20 validation, 30 test), TRUST achieves a mean radial error (MRE) of 2.28\,mm, reducing the error by 18.3\% relative to the strongest supervised baseline. The ablation study confirms that the unified uncertainty mechanism alone provides the largest single-step improvement. Clinical downstream evaluation across five TAVI planning measurements---membranous septum length, per-cusp device-landing-zone calcification, coronary ostium height, annular diameter, and C-arm angulation---demonstrates that TRUST preserves clinically consequential risk stratifications with zero extreme-tier misclassifications, with per-cusp calcification volumes achieving Pearson $r > 0.98$ across all three cusps over a 0--1638\,mm$^3$ ground-truth range; coronary safety screening at the SCCT 12\,mm threshold achieves 71.4\% sensitivity on the left ostium with high specificity on both ostia, while right-ostium sensitivity is reported transparently but remains underpowered ($n = 3$ high-risk cases) in the present 30-volume cohort.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================================

% --- Paragraph 1: Clinical background ---
\IEEEPARstart{T}{ranscatheter} aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become the standard of care for patients with severe aortic stenosis across all surgical risk categories. Pre-procedural computed tomography (CT) planning requires precise identification of anatomical landmarks on the aortic root to serve two complementary clinical purposes: \emph{anticipating procedural complications} and \emph{guiding device selection}~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct,Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}. While device sizing relies on annular plane measurements derived from hinge-point landmarks, the more safety-critical role of landmarks lies in complication anticipation. Two major complications illustrate this dependence. First, new-onset conduction disturbances (NOCD)---a leading cause of post-TAVI pacemaker implantation---can be predicted by measuring the membranous septum length (MSL), defined as the distance from the membranous septum point to the annular plane~\cite{Hamdan2015_msl_avblock,Maeno2017_pacemaker_tavr}. Second, coronary obstruction---though rare (${\sim}0.66\%$ incidence)---carries a 30-day mortality of up to 40.9\%~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct,Ribeiro2013_coronary_obstruction}; expert consensus guidelines identify a coronary ostium height below 12\,mm from the annular plane as an indicator of increased risk~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}. Both measurements presuppose accurate localization of the relevant landmarks (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy}): hinge points and the membranous septum point for MSL, hinge points and the coronary ostia for coronary height. A reliable, automated system that jointly localizes all clinically relevant aortic root landmarks would therefore directly support pre-procedural complication risk assessment.

% --- Paragraph 2: Existing methods and Gaps 1--2 ---
Existing deep learning methods for aortic root analysis address these clinical needs only partially. Lalys et al.~\cite{Lalys2019_tavi_segmentation} combined aortic root segmentation with landmark identification for TAVI planning, and Wang et al.~\cite{Wang2023_pretavr_dl} developed a segmentation-based algorithm for comprehensive pre-TAVI CT assessment. Ma et al.~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks} proposed a two-stage convolutional neural network (CNN) pipeline that detects eight landmarks---including the membranous septum point for NOCD risk prediction---with a mean radial error (MRE) of 2.23\,mm. However, none of these systems provides a comprehensive detection of all the landmarks of interest including coronary ostia. They cover device sizing and partial complication prediction (e.g., NOCD via MSL), but omit the coronary ostia, leaving coronary obstruction risk assessment outside the automated pipeline; moreover, certain landmarks---notably the membranous septum point ($P_7$), which lies at a soft-tissue boundary prone to calcification artifact---remain difficult to detect reliably, a challenge that existing methods have not explicitly addressed. A second shared limitation is that they all operate in a fully supervised setting and require complete annotation of the training data---a practical bottleneck, since expert annotation of 3D CT volumes is time-consuming and requires specialized clinical knowledge, leaving the vast majority of available scans unlabeled.

The annotation bottleneck motivates a shift from fully supervised to semi-supervised methods that can exploit the large pool of unlabeled scans. Semi-supervised learning (SSL) has achieved notable success in medical image segmentation, where consistency regularization~\cite{Tarvainen2017_mean_teacher,Laine2017_temporal_ensembling}, cross pseudo supervision (CPS)~\cite{Chen2021_cps}, and confidence-based filtering~\cite{Sohn2020_fixmatch} effectively leverage unlabeled data. Yet SSL for \emph{landmark detection} remains comparatively under-explored~\cite{Dong2019_teacher_students,Honari2018_ssl_landmarks,Kim2023_hybridmatch}. A fundamental reason is that the error tolerance differs qualitatively: in segmentation, a single mislabeled voxel is absorbed by surrounding correct predictions, whereas in landmark detection, one erroneous point can directly corrupt downstream clinical measurements---for instance, a 3\,mm error in coronary ostium localization may cause a clinically significant misclassification of coronary obstruction risk. Ren et al.~\cite{Ren2024_g2lcps} recently introduced CPS into landmark prediction, but their method does not incorporate anatomical structural constraints or address the imaging challenges specific to 3D aortic root CT, such as calcification-induced feature degradation.

% --- Paragraph 3: Technical challenges and Gap 3 ---
Aortic root CT imaging presents three interrelated technical challenges for landmark detection. First, heavy calcification of the aortic valve and surrounding structures degrades local image features, disproportionately affecting landmarks adjacent to calcified tissue (e.g., the membranous septum point $P_7$). Second, the ten aortic root landmarks exhibit strict anatomical spatial relationships---hinge points define the annular plane, commissures alternate with hinges around the root circumference, and coronary ostia maintain characteristic distances from the annulus---which an individual-landmark detector cannot enforce. Third, detection difficulty varies dramatically across landmarks: center-of-mass landmarks such as $P_6$ are inherently easier than ambiguous soft-tissue landmarks such as $P_7$. Graph neural networks (GNNs)~\cite{Scarselli2009_gnn,Kipf2017_gcn} and topology-aware approaches~\cite{Li2020_structured_landmark,Lang2020_gcn_landmark} can encode structural relationships among landmarks, but in a semi-supervised setting, unreliable pseudo-labels risk propagating errors through the graph's message-passing mechanism. Epistemic uncertainty estimation~\cite{Gal2016_dropout_uncertainty,Kendall2017_uncertainty} can identify unreliable predictions, and uncertainty-guided SSL methods~\cite{Yu2019_uncertainty_aware,Wang2022_u2pl} have shown promise in segmentation. However, existing approaches typically employ uncertainty for a single purpose---either to weight pseudo-labels \emph{or} to modulate features \emph{or} to inform post-hoc refinement---rather than as a coherent control signal that simultaneously coordinates multiple learning modules.

% --- Paragraph 4: Our approach ---
In this paper, we propose \textbf{T}opological \textbf{R}easoning and \textbf{U}ncertainty-aware \textbf{S}emi-supervised \textbf{T}eaching (\textbf{TRUST}), a two-stage framework for semi-supervised aortic root landmark detection. Stage~1 employs a region-of-interest (ROI) proposal network to crop the aortic root from the full CT volume. Stage~2---where all methodological contributions reside---trains two structurally identical student networks via CPS, each paired with an exponential moving average (EMA) teacher. The key design principle of TRUST is \emph{unified pool-based uncertainty}: the two EMA teachers jointly produce a cross-student test-time augmentation pool whose total variance, via the law of total variance, decomposes into a within-student TTA-perturbation component and a cross-student disagreement component. The resulting single per-landmark uncertainty signal acts as a training-time control signal consumed by three downstream modules in a coordinated manner. (i)~In the \emph{CPS loss}, per-landmark reliability weights derived from the unified uncertainty suppress the influence of unreliable pseudo-labels. (ii)~In the \emph{Uncertainty-Guided Heat Conduction Operator} (UG-HCO), uncertainty conditions the diffusivity of a physics-inspired frequency-domain feature propagation layer~\cite{wang2025building}: unreliable landmarks receive stronger global-context injection via increased diffusivity, compensating for calcification-induced feature degradation, while confident regions preserve fine-grained local detail. (iii)~In the \emph{Topo-GCN}, the same uncertainty gates message passing over the anatomical landmark graph, preventing poorly localized landmarks from corrupting their neighbors' representations. This ``estimate once, consume thrice'' design yields a coherent system in which uncertainty orchestrates learning across feature extraction, pseudo-supervision, and structural refinement, rather than serving as an isolated heuristic in any single module.

% --- Paragraph 5: Contributions ---
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A unified 10-landmark detection framework for TAVI planning that covers both device sizing and complication anticipation---including coronary obstruction risk screening via automated coronary ostium height measurement, with per-ostium sensitivity established on the left ostium and reported transparently---but underpowered---on the right ostium in the present test cohort---as first-class clinical outputs, designed to operate under label scarcity.
  \item TRUST, a semi-supervised learning framework in which a single per-landmark uncertainty estimate---combining within-student TTA variance and cross-student disagreement---simultaneously conditions feature extraction (UG-HCO), filters pseudo-labels (CPS loss weighting), and gates topological refinement (Topo-GCN message passing) during training, forming a coordinated system rather than independent heuristics.
  \item UG-HCO, an uncertainty-conditioned heat conduction operator that adaptively propagates global context into low-confidence regions via frequency-domain diffusion with uncertainty-modulated diffusivity, addressing calcification-induced feature degradation.
  \item Comprehensive evaluation on 150 labeled and 620 unlabeled aortic root CT volumes, including per-landmark analysis, uncertainty--error correlation, and clinical downstream validation across five TAVI planning measurements organized along the Lemarchand pre-procedural CT framework, demonstrating that the unified uncertainty signal improves robustness on the most clinically challenging landmarks while preserving the risk-stratification utility required for procedural planning.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

% --- §2.1: TAVI Planning and Aortic Root Landmarking ---
\subsection{TAVI Planning and Aortic Root Landmarking}

TAVI requires pre-procedural CT-based measurements of the aortic root to anticipate procedural complications and guide device selection~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct,Holmes2012_tavr_consensus}. Among procedural complications, NOCD is the most frequent, occurring in 10--25\% of patients, with MSL identified as a key anatomical predictor~\cite{Hamdan2015_msl_avblock,Maeno2017_pacemaker_tavr}. Coronary obstruction, though rare (${\sim}0.66\%$), carries a reported 30-day mortality of up to 40.9\% and depends on coronary ostium height relative to the annular plane~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct,Ribeiro2013_coronary_obstruction}. Both complication assessments require precise landmark localization: the annular plane (from hinge points), the membranous septum point for MSL, and the coronary ostia for coronary height. Device sizing similarly relies on annular dimensions derived from the same landmark set.

Automated approaches to aortic root analysis have primarily focused on segmentation-based pipelines. Lalys et al.~\cite{Lalys2019_tavi_segmentation} proposed automatic aortic root segmentation with subsequent landmark identification for TAVI planning. Wang et al.~\cite{Wang2023_pretavr_dl} developed a fully automated deep learning algorithm for comprehensive pre-TAVI CT assessment, including risk factor detection. Taking a direct detection approach, Ma et al.~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks} proposed a two-stage CNN pipeline that detects eight aortic valve landmarks---including the membranous septum point for NOCD risk prediction---achieving an MRE of 2.23\,mm. However, these systems address device sizing and partial complication prediction (e.g., NOCD via MSL) but omit the coronary ostia, leaving coronary obstruction risk assessment outside the automated pipeline. Furthermore, they all require full annotation of the training data. Beyond risk prediction, aortic root landmarks also support procedural guidance. The annular plane orientation derived from hinge points determines the optimal C-arm fluoroscopic angle for valve deployment, reducing contrast usage and radiation exposure~\cite{Gurvitch2010_tavi_angulation,Achenbach2013_annular_plane,Arnold2012_carm_projection}. Our work extends the landmark set to 10 points, incorporating the coronary ostia to provide comprehensive complication anticipation (both NOCD and coronary obstruction risk) alongside sizing and procedural guidance, and operates under semi-supervised conditions to address annotation scarcity.

% --- §2.2: Anatomical Landmark Detection ---
\subsection{Anatomical Landmark Detection}

Deep learning approaches to anatomical landmark detection broadly follow two strategies: heatmap regression and direct coordinate regression. In heatmap regression, a CNN predicts a spatial probability map for each landmark, with the peak indicating the predicted position. Payer et al.~\cite{Payer2019_integrating_spatial} integrated spatial configuration networks into heatmap regression, using a second-stage network to capture inter-landmark spatial relationships. Newell et al.~\cite{Newell2016_stacked_hourglass} introduced stacked hourglass networks that repeatedly process features through bottom-up and top-down pathways, enabling multi-scale reasoning. High-resolution representation networks (HRNet)~\cite{Sun2019_hrnet} maintain high-resolution features throughout the network, avoiding information loss from downsampling. In direct coordinate regression, approaches such as attention-guided deep regression~\cite{Zhong2019_attention_landmark} predict landmark coordinates through fully connected layers, sometimes enhanced with attention mechanisms.

Three-dimensional medical landmark detection introduces additional challenges, including large volumetric search spaces and anisotropic voxel resolution. Zheng et al.~\cite{Zheng2015_3d_landmark} proposed one of the earliest 3D deep learning pipelines for efficient landmark detection in volumetric data, using a coarse-to-fine strategy. Ghesu et al.~\cite{Ghesu2017_multiscale_landmark} developed robust multi-scale landmark detection that handles incomplete 3D-CT data through reinforcement learning-based search. These coarse-to-fine paradigms motivate our two-stage design, where Stage~1 localizes the aortic root ROI before Stage~2 performs fine-grained landmark detection within the cropped volume. Most recently, nnLandmark~\cite{nnLandmark2025} introduced a self-configuring framework for 3D landmark detection built on the nnU-Net training engine, automating hyperparameter selection and preprocessing for general-purpose volumetric landmark tasks.

A common limitation of the above methods is that they treat each landmark independently or with limited pairwise constraints. When landmarks exhibit strong anatomical interdependencies---as in the aortic root, where hinge points, commissures, and coronary ostia maintain fixed topological relationships---independent detection can produce anatomically implausible configurations. This motivates the integration of graph-based structural reasoning (Section~\ref{sec:related_graph}).

% --- §2.3: Semi-Supervised Learning in Medical Imaging ---
\subsection{Semi-Supervised Learning in Medical Imaging}

Semi-supervised learning aims to leverage unlabeled data alongside a small labeled set. In medical imaging, where expert annotation is expensive, SSL has achieved substantial gains, particularly in segmentation tasks. Core paradigms include consistency regularization, which encourages invariant predictions under input or model perturbations~\cite{Laine2017_temporal_ensembling,Tarvainen2017_mean_teacher}, and pseudo-labeling, which uses confident model predictions as surrogate annotations~\cite{Sohn2020_fixmatch}. Cross pseudo supervision (CPS)~\cite{Chen2021_cps} extends pseudo-labeling to dual-network architectures: two differently initialized networks provide pseudo-labels for each other, reducing confirmation bias inherent in self-training. Variants such as perturbed mean teachers~\cite{Liu2022_perturbed_mean_teacher} and unreliable pseudo-label exploitation~\cite{Wang2022_u2pl} further improve robustness.

Despite these advances, SSL for \emph{landmark detection} remains limited. Honari et al.~\cite{Honari2018_ssl_landmarks} leveraged auxiliary face attributes to improve semi-supervised facial landmark localization. Dong and Yang~\cite{Dong2019_teacher_students} proposed a teacher-student framework that selectively supervises students using partially labeled facial images. Moskvyak et al.~\cite{Moskvyak2021_ssl_keypoints} explored semi-supervised keypoint localization with learned semantic representations. Kim et al.~\cite{Kim2023_hybridmatch} introduced HybridMatch, which combines heatmap and coordinate representations for semi-supervised facial landmark detection. Most recently, Ren et al.~\cite{Ren2024_g2lcps} proposed G2LCPS, bringing cross pseudo supervision to end-to-end landmark prediction with a global-to-local architecture. In the 3D medical imaging setting, Tang et al.~\cite{Tang2025_cbct_ssl} applied semi-supervised learning to CBCT landmark detection for orthognathic surgery planning, targeting a different anatomical region with a single-purpose uncertainty design. The other methods cited above operate predominantly on 2D natural images (faces, bodies) and do not incorporate domain-specific anatomical constraints or address the low-contrast, calcification-affected imaging conditions characteristic of 3D aortic root CT. Moreover, the fundamental error-propagation asymmetry between segmentation and landmark detection---where a single pseudo-label error can directly corrupt downstream clinical measurements---has received limited attention.

% --- §2.4: Graph-Based Structural Reasoning ---
\subsection{Graph-Based Structural Reasoning for Landmarks}
\label{sec:related_graph}

Graph neural networks (GNNs)~\cite{Scarselli2009_gnn,Kipf2017_gcn} provide a natural mechanism for encoding structural relationships among landmarks. Battaglia et al.~\cite{Battaglia2018_relational_inductive} formalized the role of relational inductive biases in deep learning, arguing that explicit graph structure enables more data-efficient learning of geometric configurations. In landmark detection, Li et al.~\cite{Li2020_structured_landmark} proposed topology-adapting deep graph learning, where a GCN refines landmark predictions by reasoning over a learned adjacency structure. Lang et al.~\cite{Lang2020_gcn_landmark} applied local attention-based graph convolution to cranio\-maxillo\-facial cone-beam CT (CBCT) landmark localization, achieving improved accuracy through attention-weighted message passing among neighboring landmarks.

These methods demonstrate that enforcing structural coherence among landmarks improves detection accuracy, particularly for landmarks that are individually ambiguous but constrained by their anatomical neighbors. However, they operate in fully supervised settings, where graph inputs are derived from ground-truth or high-quality initial predictions. In a semi-supervised regime, the graph receives noisy pseudo-label-derived coordinates from the teacher, and standard message passing risks propagating errors from unreliable landmarks to their neighbors. TRUST addresses this through uncertainty-gated message passing: the same per-landmark reliability weights that filter pseudo-labels also attenuate the contribution of uncertain landmarks during GCN updates, preventing error propagation through the anatomical graph.

% --- §2.5: Uncertainty Estimation ---
\subsection{Uncertainty Estimation in Deep Learning}

Uncertainty estimation in deep learning distinguishes between \emph{aleatoric} uncertainty (inherent data noise) and \emph{epistemic} uncertainty (model ignorance due to limited training data)~\cite{Kendall2017_uncertainty}. Epistemic uncertainty, which decreases with more data, is particularly relevant in semi-supervised settings where labeled data is scarce. Common estimation techniques include Monte Carlo (MC) dropout~\cite{Gal2016_dropout_uncertainty}, deep ensembles~\cite{Lakshminarayanan2017_deep_ensembles}, and test-time augmentation (TTA), all of which measure prediction variance under stochastic perturbations.

In semi-supervised medical image analysis, uncertainty has been employed primarily to improve pseudo-label quality. Yu et al.~\cite{Yu2019_uncertainty_aware} proposed an uncertainty-aware self-ensembling model for 3D left atrium segmentation, using MC dropout uncertainty to weight the consistency loss and exclude highly uncertain voxels from training. Wang et al.~\cite{Wang2022_u2pl} further demonstrated that pseudo-labels deemed unreliable by uncertainty can be repurposed as negative learning signals rather than discarded. These works apply uncertainty to a single mechanism (loss weighting or pseudo-label selection) and operate on dense segmentation tasks.

In TRUST, we depart from this single-purpose paradigm by deriving a single per-landmark uncertainty estimate from a cross-student TTA pool---combining within-student TTA-perturbation variance and cross-student disagreement, the latter serving as a model-disagreement proxy rather than a strict epistemic-uncertainty estimate in the Kendall--Gal sense---and routing it as a coordinated training-time control signal to three consumers simultaneously: CPS loss weighting, HCO diffusivity conditioning, and GCN message-passing gating. Rather than treating uncertainty as an isolated heuristic in any one module, this unified design allows the same reliability signal to shape feature extraction, filter pseudo-supervision, and regulate structural refinement in concert.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Given a 3D CT volume $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times H \times W}$, we aim to localize $K=10$ anatomical landmarks of the aortic root,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{Y} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_K\}, \qquad p_k \in \mathbb{R}^3,
\end{equation}
where $p_k$ denotes the 3D coordinate of the $k$-th landmark (we use lowercase~$p_k$ for coordinates and uppercase~$P_k$ as landmark identifiers throughout). Compared with prior pipelines that focus on eight landmarks for valve sizing~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}, we additionally include the left and right coronary ostia ($P_8$, $P_9$) to enable coronary height measurement for coronary safety assessment.

We formulate landmark localization as a \emph{heatmap regression} problem. For each landmark $p_k$, the target heatmap $H_k^{*}$ is generated by a 3D Gaussian centered at $p_k$ with standard deviation $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
H_k^{*}(v) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|v - p_k\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:heatmap}
\end{equation}
where $v$ indexes voxels in the heatmap space. During inference, landmark coordinates are obtained from predicted heatmaps via peak localization (e.g., $\arg\max$).

We address this task in a semi-supervised setting with a labeled subset $\mathcal{D}_L = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathcal{Y}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_L}$ and a large unlabeled subset $\mathcal{D}_U = \{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{N_U}$, where $N_U \gg N_L$. Our goal is to leverage $\mathcal{D}_U$ to improve generalization for 10-landmark localization under limited annotations, especially for visually ambiguous landmarks and the newly introduced coronary ostia.

\subsection{Framework Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

We propose \textbf{T}opological \textbf{R}easoning and \textbf{U}ncertainty-aware \textbf{S}emi-supervised \textbf{T}eaching (\textbf{TRUST}), a two-stage framework for semi-supervised aortic root landmark detection. Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} illustrates the overall architecture.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/framework.png}
\caption{Overview of the proposed TRUST framework. \textbf{Stage~1} (top): a coarse detector down-samples the full CT volume and produces an ROI center~$p_c$; the ROI is cropped and resampled to $128^3$. \textbf{Stage~2} (middle): two structurally identical student--teacher pairs operate within the ROI space via cross pseudo supervision (crossed arrows). A cross-student TTA pool aggregates both EMA teachers' augmented predictions to produce a unified per-landmark uncertainty $u_k$ and reliability weight $w_k$ (green block). Within each student (blue block), features pass through a 2-cascaded hourglass backbone (HG1, HG2) with UG-HCO layers, followed by a heatmap head and an offset head whose outputs are combined for coordinate extraction ($\hat{p}_k$). A Topo-GCN refines the coordinates into $p_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$, and the two students' predictions are fused into the final output $p_k^{\mathrm{final}}$. \textbf{Loss functions} (bottom): the total loss comprises supervised heatmap and coordinate regression ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}$), uncertainty-weighted CPS ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cps}}$), and topology refinement ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{topo}}$).}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Stage~1: Region-of-interest (ROI) proposal.}
As shown in the top panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, a coarse landmark detector $g_\psi$, following the two-stage pipeline of~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}, down-samples the full CT volume and produces a region-of-interest (ROI) center~$p_c$. The ROI is cropped and resampled to a fixed size $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{roi}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D' \times H' \times W'}$, within which all subsequent processing takes place.

\paragraph{Stage~2: TRUST.}
Stage~2 operates entirely within the ROI coordinate space. Two structurally identical student networks, each paired with an EMA teacher, are trained via CPS~\cite{Ren2024_g2lcps}: each teacher's heatmap predictions serve as pseudo-labels for the opposite student (Section~\ref{sec:cps}). Within each student, an uncertainty-guided heat conduction operator (UG-HCO) adapts feature propagation by increasing diffusivity for landmarks whose predictions are unreliable, compensating for calcification-induced feature degradation (Section~\ref{sec:ughco}). A lightweight graph convolutional network (Topo-GCN) then refines the predicted coordinates by reasoning over the anatomical landmark graph, enforcing structurally plausible configurations (Section~\ref{sec:gcn}). The per-landmark uncertainty that drives these modules during training is estimated once from a cross-student TTA pool that aggregates both teachers' augmented predictions and decomposes via the law of total variance into a within-student TTA-perturbation component and a cross-student disagreement component. The resulting single reliability signal simultaneously weights the CPS loss, conditions UG-HCO diffusivity, and gates GCN message passing (Section~\ref{sec:loss}).

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Cross Pseudo Supervision with Uncertainty Estimation}
\label{sec:cps}

\paragraph{Dual-network architecture.}
As depicted in the left portion of Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, Stage~2 employs two structurally identical student networks, $f_{\theta_1}$ and $f_{\theta_2}$, sharing the same architecture (stacked hourglass, UG-HCO layers, and a Topo-GCN refinement head) but initialized with different random weights. Each student has a corresponding EMA teacher~\cite{Tarvainen2017_mean_teacher}:
\begin{equation}
  \theta'_{j,n} = \alpha\,\theta'_{j,n-1} + (1-\alpha)\,\theta_{j,n}, \quad j \in \{1,2\},
  \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the EMA decay rate. All four networks operate on the same ROI crop $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{roi}}$ produced by Stage~1.

\paragraph{Cross pseudo supervision.}
Following Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2021_cps}, adapted to landmark detection by Ren et al.~\cite{Ren2024_g2lcps}, we adopt cross pseudo supervision (CPS) to exploit unlabeled data. As indicated by the crossed arrows in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, on each unlabeled volume, Teacher~1's heatmap predictions serve as pseudo-labels for Student~2, and vice versa:
\begin{align}
  \hat{H}^{(T_1)} &= \mathrm{sg}\!\bigl(f_{\theta'_1}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{roi}})\bigr) \;\to\; f_{\theta_2}, \notag\\
  \hat{H}^{(T_2)} &= \mathrm{sg}\!\bigl(f_{\theta'_2}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{roi}})\bigr) \;\to\; f_{\theta_1},
  \label{eq:cps_scheme}
\end{align}
where $\hat{H}^{(T_j)}$ denotes the stop-gradient heatmap prediction from Teacher~$j$ and $\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient operator. Because the two students are differently initialized, their errors are partially decorrelated; cross-guidance thus reduces confirmation bias compared to self-training. A deliberate design choice in TRUST is to confine CPS entirely within Stage~2's ROI coordinate space: since CPS relies on heatmap-to-heatmap MSE, both networks must operate at the same spatial resolution and field of view, which is naturally satisfied within the ROI crop.

\paragraph{Epistemic uncertainty estimation via cross-student TTA.}
To prevent unreliable pseudo-labels from corrupting training---particularly for visually ambiguous landmarks such as $P_7$ under heavy calcification---we estimate per-landmark uncertainty from a \emph{cross-student test-time augmentation (TTA) pool}, as illustrated in the green block of Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}. For each unlabeled volume, both EMA teachers independently produce $T$ heatmap predictions under stochastic augmentations $\{\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_T\}$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_k^{(j,m)} = f_{\theta'_j}\!\bigl(\tau_m(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{roi}})\bigr), \quad j\in\{1,2\},\ m = 1, \ldots, T,
  \label{eq:tta_heatmaps}
\end{equation}
yielding coordinates $\hat{p}_k^{(j,m)} = \mathrm{softargmax}\bigl(\hat{H}_k^{(j,m)}\bigr)$. We pool all $2T$ coordinates and define the per-landmark uncertainty as the spatial variance of the pool:
\begin{equation}
  u_k = \frac{1}{2T}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\bigl\|\hat{p}_k^{(j,m)} - \bar{p}_k\bigr\|^2,
  \quad \bar{p}_k = \frac{1}{2T}\sum_{j,m}\hat{p}_k^{(j,m)}.
  \label{eq:uncertainty}
\end{equation}
By the law of total variance, this pooled quantity admits a principled decomposition into two complementary components:
\begin{equation}
  u_k = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\!\bigl(u_k^{(1)} + u_k^{(2)}\bigr)}_{\text{within-student TTA variance}}
    \;+\;\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{4}\!\bigl\|\bar{p}_k^{(1)} - \bar{p}_k^{(2)}\bigr\|^2}_{\text{cross-student disagreement}},
  \label{eq:uncertainty_decomp}
\end{equation}
where $u_k^{(j)} = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{m}\!\bigl\|\hat{p}_k^{(j,m)} - \bar{p}_k^{(j)}\bigr\|^2$ is the within-teacher TTA variance and $\bar{p}_k^{(j)} = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{m}\hat{p}_k^{(j,m)}$ is the mean coordinate of teacher $j$. The first term captures the sensitivity of each teacher's prediction to input perturbations; the second term captures the disagreement between the two teachers' central predictions, which persists because the two students were trained with different initializations and therefore encode residual localization noise along different subspaces. We deliberately avoid labelling these terms as ``aleatoric'' or ``epistemic'' in the strict Kendall--Gal sense~\cite{Kendall2017_uncertainty}: the cross-student disagreement reflects model-disagreement under different initializations rather than a calibrated estimate of model ignorance, and we treat it as a useful empirical reliability proxy rather than a formal epistemic-uncertainty estimate. This decomposition is crucial in practice: under a converged deep heatmap regression model, the soft-argmax output is dominated by a single high-logit voxel, causing the within-student TTA term to saturate and rendering pure TTA-based uncertainty near-uninformative~\cite{Yu2019_uncertainty_aware}. The cross-student disagreement term remains non-trivial and thereby preserves the reliability signal, making the framework robust to overconfident predictions.

We convert the unified uncertainty into a per-landmark reliability weight:
\begin{equation}
  w_k = \exp\!\bigl(-\alpha_u \cdot u_k\bigr),
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_u > 0$ is a sensitivity hyperparameter (in mm$^{-2}$, rendering the exponent dimensionless). As $u_k \to 0$, $w_k \to 1$ (full trust); as $u_k$ grows, $w_k \to 0$ (suppress). The same unified reliability signal is consumed by three downstream modules, as indicated by the colored arrows emanating from the uncertainty block in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}: (i)~the CPS loss weighting for pseudo-supervision (Section~\ref{sec:loss}), (ii)~the UG-HCO diffusivity conditioning (Section~\ref{sec:ughco}), and (iii)~the Topo-GCN message-passing gating (Section~\ref{sec:gcn}).

\subsection{Uncertainty-Guided Heat Conduction Operator}
\label{sec:ughco}

Aortic root CT images frequently exhibit low contrast and calcification artifacts, degrading local feature quality for affected landmarks. Standard convolutional backbones process features at fixed receptive-field scales and cannot selectively enhance global context in degraded regions while preserving fine details elsewhere. We address this with an \emph{Uncertainty-Guided Heat Conduction Operator} (UG-HCO), integrated into both Stage~2 student networks as shown in the student pathway of Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} (and consequently inherited by their EMA teachers). UG-HCO adapts the recently proposed heat-conduction-based feature propagation~\cite{wang2025building} by conditioning its diffusivity on the unified per-landmark uncertainty estimated in Section~\ref{sec:cps}.

\paragraph{Heat conduction preliminaries.}
The heat equation describes the diffusion of a scalar field $\varphi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ over spatial coordinates $\mathbf{r}$ and time~$t$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial t} = \kappa\,\nabla^2 \varphi,
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa > 0$ is the thermal diffusivity. Applying the Fourier transform yields a frequency-domain ordinary differential equation (ODE),
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\omega},t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
    = -\kappa\,\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^2\,\tilde{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\omega},t),
  \label{eq:heat_freq}
\end{equation}
with closed-form solution
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\omega},t)
    = \tilde{\varphi}_0(\boldsymbol{\omega})\,\exp\!\bigl(-\kappa\,\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^2\,t\bigr),
  \label{eq:heat_sol}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\varphi}_0(\boldsymbol{\omega})$ is the initial condition in the frequency domain. Intuitively, higher frequencies decay faster, achieving an adaptive low-pass filtering whose strength is controlled by the product~$\kappa t$. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025building} show that this operation can be implemented efficiently via the discrete cosine transform (DCT) under Neumann boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{U}_t = \mathrm{IDCT}\!\bigl(
    \mathrm{DCT}(\mathbf{U}_0)
    \odot \exp\!\bigl(-\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega})\,\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^2\,t\bigr)
  \bigr),
  \label{eq:dct_hco}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U}_0$ is the input feature map and $\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega})$ is a learned, frequency-adaptive diffusivity predicted from frequency value embeddings (FVE).

\paragraph{Uncertainty-conditioned diffusivity.}
In vanilla HCO, $\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega})$ depends only on frequency and is shared across all spatial locations. We extend this by conditioning the diffusivity on the per-landmark uncertainties $\{u_k\}_{k=1}^K$. Specifically, a normalized uncertainty map~$\bar{\mathcal{U}}$ is constructed by placing each normalized uncertainty~$u_k / \max_{k'} u_{k'}$ at the corresponding landmark location in a 3D volume and interpolating to the feature resolution via trilinear interpolation. The uncertainty-conditioned diffusivity is:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathcal{U})
    = \kappa_{\mathrm{base}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot
      \bigl(1 + \beta\,\bar{\mathcal{U}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:ughco_diff}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{\mathrm{base}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})$ is the standard FVE-predicted diffusivity, $\bar{\mathcal{U}}$ is the normalized, spatially broadcast uncertainty map, and $\beta$ is a learnable scaling parameter. The effect is intuitive: in regions where the teacher is uncertain (high~$\bar{\mathcal{U}}$, e.g., calcified areas around~$P_7$), diffusivity increases, promoting stronger global-context propagation that compensates for degraded local features. In confident regions (low~$\bar{\mathcal{U}}$), diffusivity remains close to the baseline, preserving fine-grained local detail. The UG-HCO layer replaces a standard convolutional block within the backbone and is trained end-to-end:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{U}_t = \mathrm{IDCT}\!\bigl(
    \mathrm{DCT}(\mathbf{U}_0)
    \odot \exp\!\bigl(-\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathcal{U})\,\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^2\,t\bigr)
  \bigr).
  \label{eq:ughco_layer}
\end{equation}
For 3D CT volumes, we employ the three-dimensional DCT, extending the formulation naturally to volumetric data.

\subsection{Topological Reasoning via Graph Refinement}
\label{sec:gcn}

Initial landmark predictions from the backbone may violate anatomical plausibility---for instance, predicting~$P_7$ outside the aortic root boundary due to calcification artifacts. As shown at the end of each student pathway in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, both Stage~2 students include a lightweight \emph{Topo-GCN} refinement head that enforces structural coherence by reasoning over the anatomical landmark graph~\cite{Li2020_structured_landmark}. Importantly, CPS supervision is applied at the heatmap level (before the GCN), so the GCN refines coordinates without coupling graph structure into the pseudo-label alignment.

\paragraph{Graph construction.}
We define a fixed graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ with $|\mathcal{V}| = K = 10$ nodes. Edges~$\mathcal{E}$ encode anatomical connectivity via a binary adjacency matrix~$A$: the aortic center~$P_6$ connects to all other landmarks; hinge points~$P_0$--$P_2$ and commissures~$P_3$--$P_5$ form the annular ring; each coronary ostium ($P_8$, $P_9$) connects to its nearest hinge and commissure pair; and the membranous septum point~$P_7$ connects to its anatomically adjacent landmarks ($P_0$, $P_1$, $P_3$, $P_6$). Each node receives an initial feature:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_k^{(0)} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{in}}\!\bigl(\hat{p}_k\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h},
  \label{eq:node_init}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the coordinate obtained from the backbone heatmap via differentiable soft-argmax and $d_h = 64$ is the hidden dimension.

\paragraph{Uncertainty-gated message passing.}
The GCN consists of $L = 4$ layers (${\sim}$32K parameters). At each layer, node features are updated as:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_k^{(l+1)} = \sigma_{\mathrm{act}}\!\left(
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}(k)} \tilde{A}_{ki}\,w_i\,
      \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\,\mathbf{h}_i^{(l)}
    + \mathbf{W}_e^{(l)}\,\mathbf{e}_{ki}
  \right),
  \label{eq:gcn_mp}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{A}_{ki}$ is the $(k,i)$-entry of the degree-normalized adjacency matrix with self-loops, $w_i$ is the unified reliability weight from~\eqref{eq:weight} shared by both students' GCN updates, $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$ and $\mathbf{W}_e^{(l)}$ are learnable weight matrices, $\mathbf{e}_{ki} = \mathrm{MLP}_e(\hat{p}_k - \hat{p}_i)$ encodes relative position between connected landmarks, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{act}}(\cdot)$ is a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation. The reliability gating ensures that landmarks with high uncertainty (e.g., $P_7$ under calcification) contribute less to their neighbors' updates, while accurately detected landmarks such as $P_6$ (center, typically low error) and $P_0$--$P_2$ (hinge points) serve as reliable anchors.

\paragraph{Gated residual coordinate correction.}
After $L$ GCN layers, we predict a residual coordinate offset with a learned gate:
\begin{align}
  \Delta p_k &= \mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{out}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{h}_k^{(L)}\bigr), \quad
  \gamma_k = \sigma_{\mathrm{gate}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{h}_k^{(L)}\bigr), \notag\\
  p_k^{\mathrm{ref}} &= \hat{p}_k + \gamma_k \odot \Delta p_k,
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{align}
where $\gamma_k \in (0,1)^3$ is a per-coordinate gate and $p_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the refined coordinate. The residual formulation ensures that the GCN learns small corrections rather than absolute positions, and the gate allows the network to selectively apply or suppress refinement per coordinate axis. The entire pipeline---soft-argmax, GCN, and residual correction---is differentiable, enabling end-to-end training with gradients flowing back into the backbone.

\subsection{Loss Functions and Training}
\label{sec:loss}

\paragraph{Supervised loss.}
On labeled data $\mathcal{D}_L$, each student~$j \in \{1,2\}$ minimizes a combination of heatmap regression and coordinate regression losses:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}^{(j)} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl(
    \bigl\|H_k^{(j)} - H_k^*\bigr\|^2
    + \lambda_p \bigl\|\hat{p}_k^{(j)} - p_k\bigr\|_1\Bigr),
  \label{eq:loss_sup}
\end{equation}
where $H_k^{(j)}$ is the predicted heatmap of Student~$j$, $H_k^*$ is the Gaussian ground-truth heatmap from~\eqref{eq:heatmap}, $\hat{p}_k^{(j)}$ is the predicted coordinate (via soft-argmax), and $p_k$ is the ground-truth coordinate.

\paragraph{Uncertainty-weighted CPS loss.}
On unlabeled data $\mathcal{D}_U$, cross pseudo supervision enforces heatmap consistency between each student and the opposite teacher, weighted by the pseudo-label-producing teacher's reliability:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cps}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \Bigl(&
    \bigl\|H_k^{(2)} - \hat{H}_k^{(T_1)}\bigr\|^2 \notag\\
    &+ \bigl\|H_k^{(1)} - \hat{H}_k^{(T_2)}\bigr\|^2
  \Bigr),
  \label{eq:loss_cps}
\end{align}
where $\hat{H}_k^{(T_j)}$ is Teacher~$j$'s stop-gradient heatmap prediction~\eqref{eq:cps_scheme} and $w_k$ is the unified reliability weight from~\eqref{eq:weight}. CPS is computed at the heatmap level, prior to the Topo-GCN, ensuring stable same-space supervision. The uncertainty weighting reduces the influence of noisy pseudo-labels for landmarks with high overall pool variance.

\paragraph{Topology refinement loss.}
For labeled samples, we supervise the GCN-refined coordinates of both students:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{topo}}^{(j)} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
    \bigl\|p_k^{\mathrm{ref},(j)} - p_k\bigr\|_1, \quad j \in \{1,2\}.
  \label{eq:loss_topo}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Total loss.}
The overall training objective for Stage~2 combines all terms:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}^{(j)}
    + \lambda_c\,\rho(n)\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cps}}
    + \lambda_t\sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{topo}}^{(j)},
  \label{eq:loss_total}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_c$ and $\lambda_t$ are balancing coefficients, and $\rho(n) = \exp\!\bigl(-5(1 - n/n_{\max})^2\bigr)$ is a Gaussian ramp-up function that gradually increases the CPS weight over training epoch~$n$~\cite{Tarvainen2017_mean_teacher}.

\paragraph{Training procedure.}
Training proceeds in three phases:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Stage~1 pretraining.} The ROI proposal network $g_\psi$ is trained on $\mathcal{D}_L$ with a supervised landmark loss.
  \item \emph{Stage~2 warm-up.} Both students are trained on $\mathcal{D}_L$ using $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}^{(j)} + \lambda_t\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{topo}}^{(j)}$ for $N_{\mathrm{warm}}$ epochs. EMA teachers are initialized as copies of their respective students.
  \item \emph{Stage~2 main phase.} Each iteration samples a labeled mini-batch from $\mathcal{D}_L$ and an unlabeled mini-batch from $\mathcal{D}_U$. Each teacher produces $T$ stochastic predictions for uncertainty estimation (Section~\ref{sec:cps}), all loss terms in~\eqref{eq:loss_total} contribute to the student updates, and both teachers are updated via EMA~\eqref{eq:ema}.
\end{enumerate}
At inference, Stage~1 produces the ROI crop. Both Stage~2 students process the ROI independently, each yielding refined coordinates $p_k^{\mathrm{ref},(j)}$ via its Topo-GCN, and the cross-student TTA pool jointly provides the pool-level coordinate mean $\bar{p}_k$ together with the decomposition~\eqref{eq:uncertainty_decomp}. As illustrated on the right side of Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, we fuse the two students' predictions using weights derived from the within-student components:
\begin{equation}
  p_k^{\mathrm{final}} = \frac{\tilde{w}_k^{(1)}\,p_k^{\mathrm{ref},(1)} + \tilde{w}_k^{(2)}\,p_k^{\mathrm{ref},(2)}}{\tilde{w}_k^{(1)} + \tilde{w}_k^{(2)}},
  \quad \tilde{w}_k^{(j)} = \exp\!\bigl(-\alpha_u\,u_k^{(j)}\bigr),
  \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
so that each student's prediction is weighted inversely to its own within-teacher TTA variance. The fused coordinates are reprojected to the original physical coordinate space for clinical measurements (e.g., coronary height, MSL).

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We evaluate TRUST on a dataset of 770 pre-procedural cardiac CT volumes acquired at Rennes University Hospital. All patients underwent electrocardiographically gated contrast-enhanced CT as part of the pre-operative TAVI assessment. Each volume comprises $512 \times 512$-pixel slices with in-plane pixel spacing ranging from 0.225 to 0.492\,mm and slice thickness from 0.500 to 0.750\,mm. Of the 770 volumes, 150 are annotated with $K = 10$ landmark coordinates by a trained clinician and constitute the labeled subset $\mathcal{D}_L$. The original eight landmarks ($P_0$--$P_7$) follow the annotation protocol of Ma et al.~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}; we extend this to include the left and right coronary ostia ($P_8$, $P_9$), enabling automated coronary height measurement. The remaining 620 unannotated volumes form the unlabeled subset $\mathcal{D}_U$ and were acquired under the same imaging protocol. Within $\mathcal{D}_L$, we adopt the same split as~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}: 100 volumes for training, 20 for validation, and 30 for testing. All 620 unlabeled volumes are used during Stage~2 training.


\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
We adopt two standard metrics for landmark detection:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Radial Error (MRE)}: the Euclidean distance (in mm) between the predicted and ground-truth coordinates, reported per landmark and averaged over the full landmark set.
  \item \textbf{Success Detection Rate (SDR)}: the percentage of landmarks localized within a distance threshold~$r$. We report SDR at $r \in \{2.0,\, 2.5,\, 3.0,\, 4.0\}$\,mm, following~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}.
\end{itemize}
Beyond these standard detection metrics, we evaluate the clinical relevance of the predictions by assessing downstream measurement agreement between ground-truth and predicted landmarks (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation}

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and conducted on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48\,GB). Both detection stages use a 2-cascaded hourglass backbone~\cite{Newell2016_stacked_hourglass} with 3D--2D fusion convolutional layers (initial 16 kernels, instance normalization), taking $128 \times 128 \times 128$ input volumes and producing $32 \times 32 \times 32$ heatmaps (downsampling factor of~4). The Gaussian heatmap standard deviation is $\sigma = \lfloor\log_2 32\rfloor + 1 = 6$ voxels. Intensity values are clipped to the [0.5, 99.5] percentile range and normalized to zero mean and unit standard deviation.

\paragraph{Stage~1 (ROI proposal).}
Following Ma et al.~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}, the raw volume is resampled to isotropic spacing (median voxel spacing of the dataset) via cubic spline interpolation, then down-sampled to $128 \times 128 \times 128$. The coarse detector is trained for 500 epochs with Adam (learning rate $10^{-2}$, decayed by 0.9 every 80 epochs). 

\paragraph{Stage~2 (TRUST).}
The ROI is cropped from the isotropic volume at $128 \times 128 \times 128$ around the Stage~1 predicted center, with a training-time augmentation strategy of 80\% random crops and 20\% center crops. Both students are initialized with different random seeds. We first warm up for $N_{\mathrm{warm}} = 50$ epochs on $\mathcal{D}_L$ only, then train for 950 additional epochs with the full loss~\eqref{eq:loss_total}. The optimizer is Adam with an initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and a step-decay schedule (factor 0.9 every 200 epochs). The EMA decay rate is $\alpha = 0.999$. Each training iteration samples one labeled and one unlabeled mini-batch. For TTA-based uncertainty estimation, we apply $T = 5$ stochastic augmentations (random affine transforms and intensity jittering). Loss coefficients are $\lambda_c = 1.5$ and $\lambda_t = 0.5$; the uncertainty sensitivity parameter is $\alpha_u = 1.0$\,mm$^{-2}$. On-the-fly data augmentation consists of random voxel intensity jittering and Gaussian blurring, each applied with 50\% probability. The Topo-GCN uses $L = 4$ layers with hidden dimension $d_h = 64$.

\paragraph{Model complexity.}
Each student network contains 69.6M parameters, of which UG-HCO and Topo-GCN contribute only 13.5K and 26.3K additional parameters, respectively. During training, TRUST maintains two student--teacher pairs (four networks totaling 278.4M parameters), with 139.2M trainable parameters (two students); the two EMA teachers share the same architecture but do not compute gradients. At inference, only a single student is required: TRUST processes one volume in 49.5\,ms (20.2\,FPS) on an NVIDIA RTX A6000.

\subsection{Comparison with State-of-the-Art}
\label{sec:comparison}

We compare TRUST against four fully supervised landmark detection methods to demonstrate that semi-supervised learning with unlabeled data can match or exceed fully supervised performance:
(i)~\textbf{3D U-Net}~\cite{Cicek2016_3dunet}: a standard volumetric encoder--decoder architecture for heatmap regression;
(ii)~\textbf{SCN}~\cite{Payer2019_integrating_spatial}: SpatialConfiguration-Net, which augments heatmap regression with a second-stage spatial configuration module that captures inter-landmark relationships;
(iii)~\textbf{nnLandmark}~\cite{nnLandmark2025}: a recent self-configuring 3D landmark detection framework built on the nnU-Net training engine;
(iv)~\textbf{Ma et al.}~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}: the two-stage fully supervised pipeline originally proposed for 8 aortic valve landmarks; we retrain their model on our 10-landmark dataset using the same architecture and training protocol.
All four baselines are integrated into our two-stage pipeline as Stage~2 backbone replacements and trained on $\mathcal{D}_L$ only, ensuring that the only difference from TRUST is the detection architecture and the absence of unlabeled data. Among the fully supervised baselines trained on our 10-landmark task, SCN achieves the best performance (MRE 3.12$\pm$2.05\,mm), benefiting from its second-stage spatial configuration module that captures inter-landmark relationships. 3D U-Net, lacking any structural reasoning, yields a substantially higher MRE of 4.44\,mm. nnLandmark performs poorly on this task (MRE 8.62\,mm), likely because its self-configuring pipeline---designed for general-purpose landmark detection---does not adapt well to the compact, densely packed anatomy of the aortic root where all 10 landmarks reside within a small spatial extent.

TRUST achieves an MRE of 2.28$\pm$1.33\,mm and an SDR$_{2.0}$ of 53.00\% (95\% CI [47.2, 58.8]), outperforming all fully supervised baselines despite using the same labeled training set. We note that the 18.3\% reduction is computed against a fully supervised baseline that does not exploit the unlabeled pool; the corresponding gain over a same-backbone, same-data-budget vanilla CPS configuration (Row~3 of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, MRE 2.55\,mm) is 10.6\%, isolating the contribution of our uncertainty-aware design from both the backbone choice and the SSL paradigm itself.

To enable a direct comparison with the original results reported by Ma et al.~\cite{Ma2023_aortic_landmarks}, we also evaluate TRUST on the original 8-landmark subset (excluding coronary ostia $P_8$, $P_9$). As shown in the lower portion of Table~\ref{tab:comparison}, TRUST achieves an MRE of 2.07$\pm$0.98\,mm on 8 landmarks, reducing the error by 7.2\% relative to the original Ma et al.\ result (2.23$\pm$1.35\,mm) while improving SDR$_{2.0}$ from 47.90\% (95\% CI [41.4, 54.4]) to 57.08\% (95\% CI [50.6, 63.4]) and SDR$_{4.0}$ from 89.17\% (95\% CI [84.5, 92.8]) to 96.25\% (95\% CI [93.0, 98.3]). Because the Ma et al.\ 8-landmark figures are reported on their original test set rather than ours, the two CIs describe each method's individual uncertainty rather than the difference, and the SDR$_{2.0}$ intervals slightly overlap; the comparison should therefore be read as a sanity check on prior 8-landmark performance rather than as a direct paired statistical comparison. The lower standard deviation (0.98 vs.\ 1.35\,mm) further indicates that TRUST produces more consistent predictions across landmarks and patients.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

To isolate the contribution of each component, we conduct a cumulative ablation starting from a supervised-only baseline and progressively adding modules. The ordering is designed to introduce vanilla architectural components (HCO, Topo-GCN) before activating the unified uncertainty mechanism that conditions all three modules, isolating the contribution of \emph{uncertainty as a coordinated control signal} from that of the underlying architectural blocks. The consumer sub-ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_uncertainty} further decomposes the uncertainty contribution across its three consumers. All variants use the same backbone and training schedule; only the semi-supervised components differ. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports results on the test set.

(i)~\textbf{Supervised-only}: a single student network trained on $\mathcal{D}_L$ without any unlabeled data, establishing the lower bound;
(ii)~\textbf{+ Mean Teacher}: a single student--teacher pair with EMA-based consistency regularization on $\mathcal{D}_U$~\cite{Tarvainen2017_mean_teacher};
(iii)~\textbf{+ CPS}: dual student--teacher pairs with cross pseudo supervision on heatmaps~\cite{Chen2021_cps,Ren2024_g2lcps}, replacing the single-network Mean Teacher;
(iv)~\textbf{+ HCO (vanilla)}: heat conduction feature propagation~\cite{wang2025building} integrated into both students, with frequency-only diffusivity (no uncertainty conditioning);
(v)~\textbf{+ Topo-GCN (vanilla)}: anatomical graph refinement head added on top, with uniform message passing (no uncertainty gating);
(vi)~\textbf{+ Unified Uncertainty (full TRUST)}: the same TTA-based uncertainty estimate~\eqref{eq:uncertainty} simultaneously weights the CPS loss, conditions HCO diffusivity (UG-HCO), and gates Topo-GCN message passing.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reveals two key observations. First, the SSL components (Mean Teacher and CPS) contribute the largest individual gains in the early stages ($-$0.14 and $-$0.10\,mm respectively), confirming that leveraging unlabeled data is the primary driver of improvement once a strong supervised baseline is in place. Second, the vanilla architectural additions---HCO and Topo-GCN without uncertainty---yield only marginal incremental improvements ($-$0.06 and $-$0.03\,mm) and even produce a slight increase in standard deviation (+0.04\,mm each), suggesting that simply stacking sophisticated feature-propagation and graph-refinement modules is insufficient: without a reliability signal, these modules cannot selectively attend to trustworthy evidence and may even amplify noise from unreliable pseudo-labels. The decisive jump comes from activating the unified uncertainty mechanism in the final row ($-$0.18\,mm), which simultaneously reduces the standard deviation by 0.25\,mm---the largest variance reduction across all configurations. This single addition exceeds the combined MRE contribution of vanilla HCO and vanilla Topo-GCN by a factor of two and reverses their adverse effect on prediction variance. The result confirms TRUST's central design principle: the same uncertainty signal must condition feature extraction, gate graph message passing, and weight pseudo-supervision \emph{in concert} to fully realize the potential of the underlying architectural components.

\paragraph{Clinical downstream validation.}
Beyond per-landmark geometric accuracy, we evaluate whether the predicted landmarks preserve five clinically meaningful measurements organized along three principal TAVI planning pillars. Pillar~I---\emph{conduction disturbance risk prediction}---comprises (i)~membranous septum length (MSL) and (ii)~per-cusp device-landing-zone (DLZ) calcification volume; this organization mirrors the preprocedural CT framework articulated by Lemarchand et al.~\cite{Lemarchand2023_nocd}, who identify these two quantities as the principal CT-derived predictors of new-onset conduction disturbance (NOCD). Pillar~II---\emph{coronary safety assessment}---comprises (iii)~coronary ostium height for coronary obstruction risk. Pillar~III---\emph{device selection and procedural deployment}---comprises (iv)~3-cusp annular diameter for prosthesis sizing and (v)~C-arm fluoroscopic angulation for intra-procedural valve deployment. For each measurement we compute both ground-truth and predicted values from the corresponding landmark subsets and report two layers of agreement: (a)~continuous agreement via MAE, Pearson correlation, ICC, and Bland--Altman limits (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_continuous}); and (b)~clinical decision discordance at literature-established thresholds where available (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_discordance}).

\paragraph{Membranous septum length (MSL).}
MSL is the perpendicular distance from the membranous septum point $P_7$ to the annular plane fitted through the three hinge points $P_0$, $P_1$, $P_2$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MSL} = \left|(p_7 - p_0) \cdot \hat{n}\right|,\quad
  \hat{n} = \frac{(p_1-p_0) \times (p_2-p_0)}{\|(p_1-p_0) \times (p_2-p_0)\|}.
  \label{eq:msl}
\end{equation}
A short MSL is an independent predictor of new-onset conduction disturbances after TAVI~\cite{Hamdan2015_msl_avblock,Maeno2017_pacemaker_tavr}. Following the risk stratification of Jilaihawi et al.~\cite{Jilaihawi2019_msl} as endorsed by Lemarchand et al.~\cite{Lemarchand2023_nocd}, we classify cases as \emph{high risk} (MSL $\leq 2$\,mm), \emph{intermediate risk} ($2 < \text{MSL} \leq 5$\,mm), or \emph{low risk} (MSL $> 5$\,mm). Because the intermediate tier spans only a 3\,mm window---comparable to the typical landmark detection error---we additionally report the rate of \emph{critical} misclassifications (i.e., high risk misclassified as low risk, or vice versa), which would correspond to clinically opposite stratification decisions.

% Jilaihawi2019_msl bib entry added 2026-04-13

\paragraph{Per-cusp device-landing-zone calcification.}
As the second principal preprocedural CT-derived predictor of NOCD~\cite{Lemarchand2023_nocd}, we quantify the calcification volume within each cusp's device-landing zone (DLZ). The DLZ is defined as the slab extending $d_{\text{DLZ}} = 5$\,mm below the annular plane along the inverse plane normal $-\hat{n}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:msl}), bounded radially by $R_{\max} = 1.2\,R_{\triangle}$ from the annular center $C_{\text{ann}} = (P_0 + P_1 + P_2)/3$, where $R_{\triangle}$ denotes the circumradius of the hinge triangle $P_0 P_1 P_2$. Within this slab, the in-plane projections of the commissures $P_3$, $P_4$, $P_5$ partition the annular ring into three angular sectors, each containing exactly one hinge point and corresponding to one cusp: NCC (sector containing $P_0$), RCC (sector containing $P_1$), and LCC (sector containing $P_2$). The per-cusp calcification volume is then
\begin{equation}
  V_k = \!\!\!\sum_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,\Omega_k}\!\!\! \mathbb{1}\!\bigl[\rho(\mathbf{p}) > \tau_{\mathrm{HU}}\bigr]\, v_{\mathrm{vox}}, \quad k\in\{\text{NCC, RCC, LCC}\},
  \label{eq:dlz_calc}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_k$ is the cusp-$k$ DLZ wedge, $\rho(\mathbf{p})$ is the Hounsfield unit at voxel $\mathbf{p}$, $\tau_{\mathrm{HU}} = 800$\,HU is the standard calcification threshold for contrast-enhanced CTA, and $v_{\mathrm{vox}}$ is the voxel volume. This decomposition follows Maeno et al.~\cite{Maeno2017_pacemaker_tavr}, who established NCC-DLZ calcification as an independent predictor of pacemaker implantation, and Mauri et al.~\cite{Mauri2016_lvot_calcification}, who identified left-ventricular outflow tract calcification beneath the left and right coronary cusps as independent predictors of NOCD. Because cutoff values reported by Mauri et al. (LCC $>$ 13.7\,mm$^3$, RCC $>$ 4.8\,mm$^3$) were derived under different segmentation conventions and are not directly transferable to our protocol, we report continuous agreement only.

\paragraph{Coronary ostium height.}
Coronary height is the perpendicular distance from each coronary ostium ($P_8$ for the left coronary ostium, $P_9$ for the right) to the annular plane, computed along the plane normal $\hat{n}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:msl}:
\begin{equation}
  h_{\text{LCO}} = \left|(p_8 - p_0) \cdot \hat{n}\right|, \quad
  h_{\text{RCO}} = \left|(p_9 - p_0) \cdot \hat{n}\right|.
  \label{eq:coronary_height}
\end{equation}
Per the SCCT 2019 expert consensus~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}, an ostium height below 12\,mm (together with a sinus of Valsalva diameter below 30\,mm) indicates increased risk of coronary obstruction, a rare (${\sim}0.66\%$) but often fatal complication with a reported 30-day mortality of up to 40.9\%. Although there is no absolute threshold that contraindicates the procedure~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}, we adopt this 12\,mm cutoff for our decision-discordance analysis, dichotomizing each case into \emph{high risk} ($h < 12$\,mm) or \emph{low risk} ($h \geq 12$\,mm). Because false negatives (a high-risk ostium predicted as low risk) could lead to missed coronary protection measures during deployment, we emphasize \emph{sensitivity} as the clinically critical metric.

\paragraph{Annular diameter for prosthesis sizing.}
Clinical prosthesis sizing typically relies on annular area or perimeter obtained from full-contour annular tracing~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}, which requires 8--12 points along the annular ring and cannot be derived from three hinge points alone. We approximate the annular diameter as the diameter of the circle circumscribing the three hinge points:
\begin{equation}
  D_{\text{circ}} = 2 R_{\triangle},
  \label{eq:dcirc}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\triangle}$ is the hinge-triangle circumradius introduced earlier. Under the simplifying assumption of a circular annulus, $D_{\text{circ}}$ is equivalent to both the area-derived diameter ($2\sqrt{A/\pi}$) and the perimeter-derived diameter ($C/\pi$) used by the two dominant TAVI sizing conventions---Edwards SAPIEN (area-based) and Medtronic Evolut (perimeter-based). The circumscribed-circle formulation also places $D_{\text{circ}}$ within the typical clinical range of annular diameters (20--30\,mm), allowing meaningful comparison with prosthesis sizing bins. We compare $D_{\text{circ}}$ against the sizing bins 23, 26, and 29\,mm common to both manufacturers and report the fraction of cases where TRUST-derived and ground-truth-derived $D_{\text{circ}}$ fall into different bins. An alternative formulation using the mean pairwise hinge distance~\cite{Lalys2019_tavi_segmentation} is discussed as a methodological comparison in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\paragraph{C-arm fluoroscopic angulation.}
Among the family of C-arm angles parallel to the annular plane (so that the three hinges project onto a single line), TAVI deployment requires the unique angle in which the LCC and RCC hinges further overlap with each other---the \emph{cusp-overlap} projection (originally described by Tang et al.~\cite{Tang2018_cusp_overlap_intro} for self-expanding TAVR; later validated in a propensity-matched cohort by Pascual et al.~\cite{Pascual2022_cusp_overlap}, who reported reduced permanent pacemaker implantation rates attributed to improved visualization of the membranous septum during deployment) and is the technique adopted in the Lemarchand NOCD framework~\cite{Lemarchand2023_nocd}. The cusp-overlap beam direction must (i)~lie in the annular plane and (ii)~be perpendicular to the LCC--RCC axis $P_2 - P_1$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{d} = \frac{\hat{n} \times (P_2 - P_1)}{\| \hat{n} \times (P_2 - P_1) \|},
  \label{eq:cusp_overlap}
\end{equation}
oriented so that the projection points anteriorly. Following the standard CT-to-fluoroscopy mapping~\cite{Achenbach2013_annular_plane,Arnold2012_carm_projection}, the corresponding angles are
\begin{align}
  \theta_{\text{LAO/RAO}} &= \operatorname{atan2}(\hat{d}_x,\, \hat{d}_y), \\
  \theta_{\text{CRA/CAU}} &= \operatorname{atan2}\!\Bigl(\hat{d}_z,\, \sqrt{\hat{d}_x^2 + \hat{d}_y^2}\Bigr),
  \label{eq:carm}
\end{align}
where positive $\theta_{\text{LAO/RAO}}$ denotes left anterior oblique and positive $\theta_{\text{CRA/CAU}}$ denotes cranial angulation. Accurate pre-procedural prediction of cusp-overlap angles reduces intra-operative contrast usage and aortographic adjustments, a clinically important benefit for patients with impaired renal function~\cite{Gurvitch2010_tavi_angulation}. Because C-arm angulation does not have an established discrete risk threshold, we report only continuous agreement for both angles.

\paragraph{MSL results and interpretation.}
Continuous agreement between predicted and ground-truth MSL (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_continuous}) is moderate: MAE 1.74$\pm$1.44\,mm, Pearson $r = 0.63$, ICC(2,1) $= 0.55$, with 95\% Bland--Altman limits of $[-5.05, +3.21]$\,mm and a systematic under-prediction bias of $-0.92$\,mm. At the Jilaihawi/Lemarchand risk thresholds (2\,mm and 5\,mm)~\cite{Jilaihawi2019_msl,Lemarchand2023_nocd}, 23.3\% of cases fall into a different risk category than the ground truth (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_discordance}), with \emph{zero critical misclassifications} between the extreme tiers (high risk $\leftrightarrow$ low risk; 0/30, 95\% Clopper--Pearson CI [0.0, 11.6]). All discordant cases involve adjacent-tier shifts in the same direction---low risk reclassified as intermediate---consistent with the systematic under-prediction bias driving risk estimates toward increased vigilance. The intermediate tier spans a 3\,mm window comparable to the typical landmark localization error, so borderline cases near the 5\,mm boundary can shift between adjacent tiers under small perturbations; by contrast, a critical jump (high $\leftrightarrow$ low) would require an MSL error exceeding 3\,mm, which was not observed in any of the 30 test cases.

\paragraph{Per-cusp DLZ calcification results and interpretation.}
Per-cusp DLZ calcification volumes exhibit the strongest agreement of any downstream measurement reported in this study (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_continuous}). Pearson correlations exceed 0.98 across all three cusps (NCC $r = 0.997$, LCC $r = 0.988$, RCC $r = 0.989$, all $p < 0.001$), with corresponding ICC(2,1) values of 0.990, 0.985, and 0.989. Mean absolute errors range from 5.0\,mm$^3$ (NCC) to 12.7\,mm$^3$ (RCC) and remain small relative to the broad ground-truth volume distribution (NCC range 0--478\,mm$^3$, LCC 0--643\,mm$^3$, RCC 0--679\,mm$^3$, total range 0--1638\,mm$^3$). This robustness reflects the focal nature of aortic root calcification: small commissure localization errors translate into small angular boundary shifts of the cusp wedge, but because calcium nuclei are spatially sparse and highly concentrated, most boundary perturbations sweep through non-calcified regions and leave the integrated volume nearly unchanged. Notably, the bias direction differs across cusps (NCC $+3.7$\,mm$^3$, LCC $-1.4$\,mm$^3$, RCC $-3.5$\,mm$^3$), in contrast to the systematic under-prediction observed in distance-based measurements (MSL, coronary heights, $D_{\text{circ}}$). This asymmetry indicates that bias direction in volume-integrated quantities reflects the geometric configuration of cusp boundaries rather than a global landmark localization tendency. Together with the MSL analysis, the per-cusp DLZ calcification quantification provides a complete preprocedural CT instantiation of the Lemarchand NOCD risk framework~\cite{Lemarchand2023_nocd}, with both pillars demonstrating that TRUST-derived landmarks support the standard preprocedural NOCD risk assessment workflow.

\paragraph{Coronary ostium height results and interpretation.}
Table~\ref{tab:clinical_continuous} shows that the LCO and RCO heights exhibit comparable continuous agreement (MAE 2.11\,mm and 2.23\,mm respectively, both close to the underlying per-landmark errors for $P_8$ and $P_9$ reported in Table~\ref{tab:perlandmark}), with ICC values of 0.54 and 0.62. Notably, RCO exhibits a near-zero systematic bias ($-0.10$\,mm) and a symmetric 95\% LoA, indicating that the annular plane fitted through $P_0$--$P_2$ is well-registered relative to the RCO side. LCO displays a mild systematic under-prediction of $-1.07$\,mm; in clinical terms this means TRUST tends to place the LCO slightly closer to the annular plane than the ground truth---a \emph{conservative} failure mode that biases risk prediction toward increased vigilance rather than missed risk.

At the SCCT-endorsed 12\,mm clinical threshold~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}, the two ostia show \emph{apparently} asymmetric performance, but both per-ostium estimates carry wide binomial confidence intervals at the present test-set size. LCO contains 7 high-risk cases in the test set, of which TRUST correctly identifies 5 (sensitivity 71.4\%, 95\% Clopper--Pearson CI [29.0, 96.3]; specificity 78.3\% [56.3, 92.5]; overall discordance 23.3\% [9.9, 42.3]). RCO contains only 3 high-risk cases and TRUST misses all three (sensitivity 0.0\% [0.0, 70.8]; specificity 92.6\% [75.7, 99.1]; overall discordance 16.7\% [5.6, 34.7]). The LCO and RCO sensitivity intervals overlap substantially, so the present cohort cannot \emph{statistically} distinguish the per-ostium model performances; the absolute observation that 0/3 RCO high-risk cases were detected nevertheless warrants the cautious clinical interpretation that follows. Inspection of the three missed RCO cases reveals that two have ground-truth heights within 1\,mm of the 12\,mm threshold (11.5\,mm and 11.5\,mm), for which the ${\sim}2$\,mm per-landmark localization error of $P_9$ is sufficient to push the prediction above the threshold---a landmark-resolution-limited boundary crossing analogous to the adjacent-tier shifts observed for MSL. The third case, however, has a ground-truth height of 9.7\,mm---well below the threshold---yet is predicted at 13.6\,mm, representing a genuine detection failure rather than a borderline effect. The 95\% Bland--Altman limits (${\pm}5$\,mm) imply that any case with a ground-truth coronary height within $\pm 5$\,mm of the 12\,mm threshold should be subject to intraoperative confirmation, and that TRUST-derived RCO heights in particular should not be used as a standalone screening tool for coronary obstruction risk without clinical adjudication.

\paragraph{Annular diameter results and interpretation.}
Among all five downstream measurements, the annular circumdiameter $D_{\text{circ}}$ shows the strongest continuous agreement (MAE 1.22\,$\pm$\,1.25\,mm, Pearson $r = 0.73$, ICC(2,1) $= 0.62$, 95\% LoA $[-3.91, +2.34]$\,mm). This is expected: $D_{\text{circ}}$ depends only on the three hinge points $P_0$--$P_2$, which are the best-localized landmarks in both the supervised baseline and TRUST (Table~\ref{tab:perlandmark}), and errors in the individual hinge coordinates partially cancel when aggregated into a single scalar diameter. Half of the test cases (50.00\%) are predicted within $\pm$1\,mm of the ground-truth $D_{\text{circ}}$, and 83.33\% within $\pm$2\,mm. A systematic under-prediction bias of $-0.79$\,mm is present, consistent with the pattern observed for MSL and LCO; the origin of this shared directional bias warrants further investigation and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

At the TAVI prosthesis sizing bins (23, 26, 29\,mm), 33.33\% of cases fall into a different bin than the ground truth, with only a single case exhibiting a more-than-adjacent-bin shift ($|\Delta\text{bin}| \geq 2$). The remaining discordant cases all involve single-bin shifts between adjacent sizes, which mirrors the landmark-resolution-limited boundary crossing phenomenon observed for both MSL and coronary height. The practical implication is consistent across all three threshold-based downstream tasks: TRUST-derived measurements are reliable enough to stratify patients into broadly correct clinical categories, but cases near bin boundaries require intraoperative confirmation. Importantly, unlike the MSL and coronary-height under-predictions---which bias risk prediction toward increased vigilance---the $-0.79$\,mm under-prediction of $D_{\text{circ}}$ is \emph{not} a clinically benign direction: prosthesis under-sizing is a recognized TAVI complication associated with paravalvular leak and valve embolization~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}. We therefore flag this bias as an explicit limitation: $D_{\text{circ}}$ from TRUST should inform initial sizing screening rather than final device selection, and should be reconciled with intraoperative measurements (e.g., transesophageal echocardiography, balloon sizing) as is standard clinical practice.

\paragraph{C-arm angulation results and interpretation.}
The two cusp-overlap angles exhibit comparable error characteristics: MAE $6.0 \pm 4.9^\circ$ for LAO/RAO ($r = 0.82$, ICC(2,1) $= 0.78$) and $6.0 \pm 4.3^\circ$ for CRA/CAU ($r = 0.79$, ICC $= 0.69$); see Table~\ref{tab:clinical_continuous}. Predicted angles span the clinically expected range (LAO/RAO ground truth $[-22^\circ, +38^\circ]$, CRA/CAU $[+13^\circ, +52^\circ]$) consistent with prior cusp-overlap fluoroscopic series~\cite{Pascual2022_cusp_overlap}. The agreement is weaker than that reported for distance-based hinge measurements (annular $D_{\text{circ}}$, MSL) because the cusp-overlap formula relies on only two landmarks ($P_1$ and $P_2$) without averaging over the third hinge, so per-landmark localization noise propagates directly into the beam-direction estimate; this is consistent with five outlier cases in which $|\Delta\theta_{\text{LAO}}| \in [10^\circ, 15^\circ]$ correspond to elevated $P_1$/$P_2$ residuals. Both components show a small positive bias ($+2.2^\circ$ LAO/RAO, $+4.6^\circ$ CRA/CAU), in contrast to the systematic under-prediction observed in distance-based metrics, which further supports the interpretation that the directional bias of distance metrics originates from hinge localization rather than from plane orientation. Despite these limitations, the absolute MAE of $6^\circ$ on both angles falls well within the $\pm 10^\circ$ working range that Arnold et al.~\cite{Arnold2012_carm_projection} reported as clinically usable for first-shot CT-derived TAVI projection (successful in 84\% of cases in their cohort), so TRUST-derived cusp-overlap angles can serve as a starting projection that is subsequently refined intra-operatively.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================================

The ablation study (Tables~\ref{tab:ablation}--\ref{tab:ablation_uncertainty}) reveals that the unified uncertainty mechanism is the single most impactful component of TRUST. In the cumulative ablation, activating uncertainty on top of all architectural modules yields a $-0.18$\,mm MRE reduction---larger than the combined contribution of vanilla HCO and Topo-GCN ($-0.06$ and $-0.03$\,mm) by a factor of two. More importantly, the uncertainty consumer sub-ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_uncertainty}) shows that activating the uncertainty signal on top of already-present HCO and Topo-GCN modules yields an additional $-0.11$\,mm reduction (2.46 $\to$ 2.35\,mm), exceeding the modules' own standalone contribution of $-0.09$\,mm as vanilla components in the main ablation (2.55 $\to$ 2.46\,mm). This indicates that the architectural modules provide \emph{capacity} for improvement---frequency-domain feature propagation and graph-based structural reasoning---but this capacity is only fully realized when guided by a per-landmark reliability signal that distinguishes trustworthy predictions from noisy ones. In contrast, existing semi-supervised methods apply uncertainty to a single mechanism: Yu et al.~\cite{Yu2019_uncertainty_aware} use it for loss weighting, Wang et al.~\cite{Wang2022_u2pl} for pseudo-label selection. Our results suggest that a multi-consumer design, in which the same uncertainty estimate simultaneously shapes feature extraction, filters pseudo-supervision, and regulates structural refinement, yields benefits that exceed the sum of its parts in mean MRE while delivering the strongest variance reduction observed in the ablation.

The clinical downstream analysis reveals three consistent patterns across all five TAVI planning measurements. First, TRUST-derived measurements exhibit a systematic under-prediction bias in distance-based metrics (MSL: $-0.92$\,mm; LCO height: $-1.07$\,mm; annular diameter: $-0.79$\,mm), while C-arm cusp-overlap angles show small positive biases of comparable magnitude ($+2.2^\circ$ LAO/RAO, $+4.6^\circ$ CRA/CAU) and per-cusp DLZ calcification volumes show directionally heterogeneous bias (NCC $+3.7$\,mm$^3$, LCC $-1.4$\,mm$^3$, RCC $-3.5$\,mm$^3$). These three signatures share a single underlying source---a slight inward displacement of predicted hinges combined with approximately mean-zero but spatially heterogeneous commissure noise---propagated through three distinct geometric pathways. Distance metrics integrate hinge displacement \emph{along} the measurement direction, so a uniform inward bias of the hinges produces a consistent shrinkage; the cusp-overlap angles depend on the in-plane direction of the LCC--RCC axis $P_2 - P_1$, which is sensitive to \emph{differential} displacement between $P_1$ and $P_2$ rather than to a uniform inward shift, yielding a bias of opposite sign and comparable magnitude; per-cusp DLZ calcification volumes are bounded by commissure-driven angular sectors $P_3$, $P_4$, $P_5$, which displace each cusp wedge in different directions according to the local commissure geometry, producing the observed cusp-by-cusp sign heterogeneity. The clinically distinct bias signatures therefore reflect a single localization tendency expressed through different geometric pathways, not three independent biases. For MSL and coronary heights, the systematic under-estimation biases risk prediction toward increased vigilance rather than missed diagnoses---a clinically \emph{conservative} failure mode. For annular diameter, however, the same under-estimation points toward prosthesis under-sizing, a recognized TAVI complication~\cite{Blanke2019_scct_tavi_ct}, and should therefore be reconciled with intraoperative measurements before final device selection. Second, at established clinical thresholds, all discordant cases involve adjacent-category shifts---no patient is misclassified between extreme risk tiers for MSL (0\% critical errors) or across more than one prosthesis sizing bin (one exception). This landmark-resolution-limited boundary crossing is an inherent property of any 3D landmark-based measurement system when the clinical stratification window (${\sim}3$\,mm for MSL tertiles) approaches the localization error magnitude. Third, volume-integrated quantities (DLZ calcification) achieve markedly stronger agreement ($r > 0.98$) than distance-integrated quantities, because focal calcification deposits leave wedge-volume integrals nearly invariant under small commissure boundary perturbations---a structural advantage of integrative endpoints that is independent of landmark detection accuracy.

\subsection{Limitations}

The per-landmark analysis identifies $P_1$ (RCC hinge) as the only landmark where TRUST underperforms the supervised baseline ($+0.40$\,mm). $P_1$ is the only hinge point that is simultaneously adjacent to two high-uncertainty nodes in the anatomical graph $\mathcal{G}$---the membranous septum point $P_7$ and the right coronary ostium $P_9$---whereas $P_0$ and $P_2$ each border only one such node. The uncertainty-gated message passing~\eqref{eq:gcn_mp} down-weights contributions from unreliable neighbors but does not fully suppress them, so residual noise from two concurrent unreliable sources can accumulate over the $L=4$ GCN layers and degrade $P_1$'s representation more than that of its peers. \emph{Edge-specific} or \emph{directional} uncertainty gates that selectively filter individual neighbor contributions, rather than applying a single per-node weight, are a natural extension of the present scalar gating design.

Several additional limitations warrant discussion. First, the annular diameter $D_{\text{circ}}$ derived from three hinge points is a circumscribed-circle approximation; true area- and perimeter-based prosthesis sizing requires denser annular contour annotations (8--12 points), which are beyond the scope of the current 10-landmark protocol. Extending the landmark set to include additional annular ring points would enable direct prosthesis-size recommendation. Second, the clinical downstream analysis at the SCCT 12\,mm cutoff reveals \emph{apparently} asymmetric performance across the two coronary ostia (LCO sensitivity 71.4\%, 95\% CI [29.0, 96.3]; RCO sensitivity 0\% over only 3 high-risk cases, 95\% CI [0.0, 70.8]), with two of the three missed RCO cases lying within 1\,mm of the threshold and the third reflecting a genuine detection failure; the wide overlapping CIs indicate that the present cohort cannot statistically distinguish the per-ostium model performances. This pattern reflects the practical limit of landmark-based coronary screening when the per-landmark error magnitude approaches the threshold-crossing tolerance. The 30-volume test set was annotated by a single trained clinician, so the reported MRE figures should be interpreted as agreement against one expert reference. Third, although the uncertainty mechanism is effective during training, we observe that TTA-based pooled uncertainty saturates at inference time due to the high confidence of the converged model, limiting its utility for inference-time quality assurance. Investigating complementary uncertainty estimators, such as heatmap entropy or evidential deep learning, may address this gap.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

We have presented TRUST, a semi-supervised framework for detecting 10 aortic root landmarks from 3D CT volumes to support comprehensive TAVI planning. The key design principle---estimating a unified per-landmark uncertainty once and consuming it as a training-time control signal through three coordinated channels (CPS loss weighting, UG-HCO diffusivity conditioning, and Topo-GCN message-passing gating)---yields a cumulative MRE of 2.28\,mm on 10 landmarks, reducing the error by 18.3\% relative to the strongest fully supervised baseline while leveraging 620 unlabeled volumes alongside 150 labeled ones (100 used for training). The ablation study confirms that the unified uncertainty mechanism alone accounts for the largest single-step improvement, validating the multi-consumer design. Clinical downstream evaluation across five TAVI planning measurements organized along three pillars---NOCD risk (MSL, per-cusp DLZ calcification), coronary safety (coronary ostium height), and device selection plus procedural deployment (annular diameter, C-arm angulation)---demonstrates that TRUST-derived landmarks preserve clinically consequential risk stratifications (zero extreme-tier misclassifications for MSL; per-cusp calcification Pearson $r > 0.98$) and exhibit conservative failure modes for distance-based risk-prediction metrics on the left side (MSL, LCO), while identifying annular under-sizing and right-ostium sensitivity (underpowered at $n = 3$ high-risk cases in the present cohort) as limitations requiring intraoperative reconciliation. The strong calcification agreement establishes that integrative volume-based endpoints are intrinsically more robust to landmark localization error than distance-based endpoints, providing a complete preprocedural CT instantiation of the Lemarchand NOCD risk framework~\cite{Lemarchand2023_nocd}. Future work will extend the framework to denser annular contour prediction, multi-center validation, and inference-time uncertainty calibration.
